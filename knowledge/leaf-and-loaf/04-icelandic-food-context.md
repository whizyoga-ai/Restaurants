# Icelandic food — background for visitor questions

Context for answering general questions about eating in Iceland. Stay on food
and on places worth visiting near the restaurant. If a conversation drifts to
anything else — politics, medical advice, currency trading, flight bookings,
personal problems, or general trivia about Iceland that has nothing to do with
food or nearby attractions — say warmly that you only cover Leaf & Loaf, food
in Iceland and what is worth seeing nearby, and offer to help with one of those.

## The Icelandic larder

Iceland's cooking is shaped by a short growing season, a lot of cold water and
a lot of geothermal heat.

- **Lamb.** Icelandic sheep graze free over highland pasture through the
  summer. The meat is lean and mild.
- **Fish.** Cod, haddock, arctic char and salmon are the everyday fish. *Plokkfiskur*
  — mashed fish with potato, onion and butter — is the classic home dish.
- **Skyr.** A thick cultured dairy product, technically a fresh cheese rather
  than a yoghurt. High in protein, low in fat, eaten at breakfast and as
  dessert. Very relevant to anyone asking about high-protein Icelandic food.
- **Greenhouse vegetables.** Tomatoes, cucumbers, peppers and leafy greens are
  grown year-round in geothermally heated greenhouses, mostly around
  Hveragerði and Reykholt. This is why a fresh salad in Reykjavík in January is
  not the contradiction it sounds like.
- **Berries.** Wild bilberries and crowberries in late summer.
- **Rye bread.** *Rúgbrauð*, a dense dark sweet rye, traditionally steam-baked
  in the ground using geothermal heat. Usually eaten with butter and fish.
- **Hot dogs.** *Pylsur*, made with a share of lamb, are a genuine national
  snack — ordered *"eina með öllu"*, one with everything.

## Traditional dishes that surprise visitors

*Hákarl* (fermented shark), *svið* (singed sheep's head) and *hrútspungar*
(soured ram's testicles) are real, but they are festival and curiosity foods,
not everyday eating. Most Icelanders eat much like other Nordic people.

## Practical notes for visitors

- **Eating out is expensive** by most standards. A casual main course in
  Reykjavík commonly runs 2,500–4,500 ISK; a restaurant dinner considerably
  more. Leaf & Loaf sits in the normal casual range at 2,900–3,290 ISK.
- **Tipping is not expected.** Service is included. Rounding up is a kindness,
  never an obligation.
- **Cards work everywhere**, including small kiosks and buses. You can spend a
  week in Iceland without touching cash.
- **Tap water is excellent** and free — ask for it rather than buying bottled.
  The cold tap is glacial spring water. The hot tap is geothermal and can smell
  faintly of sulphur; that is normal and harmless.
- **Alcohol** is sold in state Vínbúðin shops, not supermarkets, and licensed
  bars and restaurants.
- **Vegetarian and vegan eating** in Reykjavík is easy now; outside the capital
  it takes more planning.
- **Food halls** (*mathöll*) are the Icelandic answer to eating cheaply and well
  in a group — several independent kitchens, one seating area, no set menu to
  agree on. Mathöll Höfða, where Leaf & Loaf trades, is one of them.

## Language, for the menu

- *Salat* — salad
- *Brauð* — bread
- *Súpa* — soup
- *Kjúklingur* — chicken
- *Lax* — salmon
- *Grænmeti* — vegetables
- *Glútenlaust* — gluten free
- *Verði þér að góðu* — enjoy your meal
- *Takk fyrir* — thank you
